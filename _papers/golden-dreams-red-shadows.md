<em>“Are you now, or have you ever been, a member of the Communist Party?”</em>[^1] These words echoed across a nation gripped by uncertainty, paralyzing Hollywood with shadows of doubt. For some, this illustrated the end of a career; for others, it drew a clear battle line between artistry and ideology. In an era defined by mass entertainment, artistic ambition, and political paranoia, the American film industry blossomed into what is now remembered as the Golden Age of Hollywood. Spanning from the 1920s to the 1950s, this period dazzled the public with some of the most critically acclaimed Hollywood films in cinematic history—movies like <em>Vertigo</em>, <em>Casablanca</em>, and <em>Citizen Kane</em>—and introduced a constellation of Hollywood stars to the world stage. Yet beneath all its glamor and the glow of the silver screen, Hollywood was tainted by censorship, blacklists, and the chilling consequences of ideological persecution. As it soared towards cultural dominance, Hollywood’s trajectory was diverted by the abrupt intrusion of Cold War politics. The intertwined legacies of two Phillips Academy alumni—Ring Lardner Jr., a Communist and a blacklisted screenwriter, and Gerard David Schine, a government aide who helped fuel the Red Scare—reveal how the battle between artistic expression and political ideology defined the Golden Age as much as its cinematic brilliance. Before Cold War political tensions engulfed Hollywood in a web of suspicion and fear, the film industry had one objective to fulfill: to become golden. The so-called Golden Age, often nostalgically remembered for its elegance, was forged through a mix of technological and cultural progress. Known otherwise as the Classical Hollywood Cinema, Old Hollywood, and the Studio Era, this period was characterized by the consolidation of filmmaking into a powerhouse of imagination and profit.[^2] Central to this transformation in the film industry was the rise of the studio system, which, at that time, consisted of five major companies. The “Big Five,” as they were called, was composed of MGM, Paramount, Fox, Warner Bros., and RKO, perfecting the vertical integration system that controlled every aspect of a film’s production, casting, distribution, and exhibition.[^3] These studios not only owned the scripts, actors, and soundstages, but also the theaters themselves.[^4] In essence, they ran a self-sustaining empire that turned filmmaking into a profitable and meticulously managed form of art.

Just as crucial to Hollywood’s transformation were the technological breakthroughs that helped to advance cinematic storytelling. Under vertical integration, studios had the resources and control to invest heavily in innovations—from synchronized sound to Technicolor—that revolutionized the cinematic experience.[^5] From the dawn of the studio age, movies were produced without soundtracks until the arrival of synchronized sound in the late 1920s. Silent films paved their way to “talkies,” which introduced spoken dialogue, ambient noise, and musical scores to connect more emotionally and intellectually with audiences.[^6] By the 1930s, sound and music became the industry standards, evolving the way stories were told on screen.[^7] Meanwhile, Technicolor—which brought lush, saturated palettes to the screen—was used to enhance the visual grandeur of cinema. Films like <em>The Wizard of Oz</em> and <em>Gone with the Wind</em> used vivid color palettes to transport audiences into a dreamlike and emotionally charged world.[^8] Critics at that time in 1934, including Andrew Sennwald, described early Technicolor as both “dramatically tedious” and “incredibly thrilling,” reflecting the tension between innovation and refinement.[^9] The advent of sound and Technicolor has helped to transform cinema into an art unbounded by technical limitations, becoming a powerful motion to convey the development of American culture, aesthetics, and beauty.

Out of this thriving system emerged the iconic faces that came to define Golden Hollywood itself. Stars such as Humphrey Bogart\*, Marilyn Monroe, Katharine Hepburn, James Stewart, Ingrid Bergman, Cary Grant, Clark Gable, and Bette Davis adorned the front page of Hollywood’s most influential period. These individuals were more than just actors and actresses; they were manufactured, polished, and projected as idealized versions of American identity.[^10] They were what gave Hollywood its golden shine. Their images, performances, and off-screen personas became inseparable from the studio brands that manufactured them.[^11]

Hollywood was, for a time, the most potent cultural engine in the world—it produced a new and distinct type of modern cinema that elevated entertainment. To most Americans—and much of the globe—the Golden Age symbolized the height of artistic achievement.[^12] People flocked to theaters not just to be entertained, but also to be transported into entirely different worlds, places accessible only through the magic of film.[^13] This desire was especially intensified by the hardships of the Great Depression, when widespread poverty and unemployment left many Americans seeking relief from their daily struggles.[^14] Movies became an essential criterion for emotional refuge, reprieving people of temporary escape from grim realities. And the Golden Age offered that necessary means. Yet beneath all its stars, glossy exterior, and sparkle, a slow current of suspicion and ideological anxiety was beginning to form.

Following the Second World War, America’s political climate shifted, making Hollywood’s cultural dominance a prime target for scrutiny.[^15] In retrospect, the Golden Age was not just a blossoming of cinema but a reconfiguration amid rising political paranoia. The dream factory not only captivated the public but also became a battleground in an ideological struggle over national identity and threats to American values. This wave of political suspicion that would later consume the industry, however, was not spontaneous but rather one that stretched back decades to the First Red Scare of the 1920s. It had set the stage for the controversy that surrounded the Golden Age towards its later years, like a shadow looming over the decades to come.

The Bolshevik Revolution of 1917 saw the rise of the Soviet Union, sending shockwaves through global political ideologies and igniting upheavals and radical socialist change.[^16] At that time in the United States, bombings by anarchical groups in cities like New York and Washington, D.C., as well as revolutionary rhetoric from newly energized radicals, sparked the First Red Scare.[^17] This initial phase of anti-Communist hysteria culminated in the Palmer Raids, which were a series of government actions aimed at arresting and deporting thousands of suspected Communists.[^18] Though this panic eventually subsided amid the nation’s yearning for “normalcy” during the 1920s, it left behind a lingering imprint of radicalism and a precedent for censorship that would later begin to envelop the United States in the 1940s, especially in institutions like the film industry.[^19] In other words, the First Red Scare prompted a desire to restore pre-war social stability, moral conservatism, and political order—a principle championed by President Warren G. Harding at that time, who famously called for a “return to normalcy” after the disorder caused by World War I and the preceding First Red Scare.[^20]

This longing for national unity helped pave the way for the Motion Picture Production Code of 1934. Also known as the Hays Code, it was implemented to impose strict guidelines on the film industry. Enforced by the Production Code Administration under Joseph Breen and overseen by Will H. Hays, the Code regulated what could appear on screen, ensuring that films were “‘presentable’ and ‘safe’ for the public at large, which meant not covering or featuring certain controversial topics, themes, or actions.”[^21] Its goal was to ensure that films were carefully aligned with socially conservative values, censoring out depictions of nudity, profanity, violence, critiques of religion, and, most notably, political dissent.[^22] Breen, who diligently worked to uphold the Hays Code for more than two decades, scrutinized “every Hollywood script for offensive social, political, and sexual themes,” ensuring that both film producers and audiences adhered to a “rigidly conservative view of politics and morality.”[^23] Though not government-mandated, the Hays Code acted as a political safeguard to help protect the film industry from external interference—especially from the government. It was designed for films to project a vision of American life that did not challenge political or religious norms, preventing the influence of these on an industry that supposedly had its freedom of expression. This code also “undertook a wholesale depoliticizing of its subject matter” in an effort to reduce “oppositional politics” that defied Breen’s firm stance of holding a conservative view.[^24] Hollywood, under this framework, became a mirror of political anxieties and traditional American values, even as the nation’s political fears deepened in the years leading up to the Cold War.

The arrival of the Second Red Scare in 1947 climaxed these anxieties.[^25] This second wave of anti-communist hysteria—commonly referred to as McCarthyism—was fueled by the deteriorating U.S.-Soviet relations and the Soviets’s latter expansion into Eastern Europe. As these tensions escalated, suspicion of subversive ideologies began to infiltrate every aspect of American life, including the arts.[^26] Hollywood, with its expansive cultural influence, was seen as both a threat and a weapon capable of shaping public opinion.

While the First Red Scare had called for normalcy through the Hays Code, the Second Red Scare sought to unearth these fears at their source.[^27] Hence, the government turned sharply toward the entertainment industry, initiating investigations, enforcing blacklists, and publicly accusing individuals within the then-rising cultural industry.[^28] The fear instilled in the country was not merely that there were communists working behind the scenes, but that films could subtly propagate anti-American values. Once the factory of dreams and American idealism, Hollywood’s expressive power became suspect.

At the center of these efforts was the House Un-American Activities Committee (HUAC). Established in 1938, HUAC intended to investigate disloyalty and subversive activities in the United States, specifically directing much of its focus on Hollywood to undermine actors, directors, and screenwriters about their political beliefs and affiliations.[^29] Those who refused to cooperate—most notably the “Hollywood Ten”—were cited for contempt of Congress and subsequently blacklisted, ending many of their careers.[^30]

Caught in the crossfire were Ring Lardner Jr., a screenwriter and member of the “Hollywood Ten,” and Gerard David Schine, an aide to Senator McCarthy who actively assisted the investigations. Though both of them were alumni of Phillips Academy, they had extremely differing paths that reflected the broader scope of Hollywood’s conflict in a time of growing radical views: one stood for artistic and free expression, while the other sought to purify Hollywood from what he saw as subversion.

Ring Lardner Jr., a committed leftist and screenwriter, became one of the most vocal figures caught in the ideological fright of mid-20th-century Hollywood. As a member of the notorious “Hollywood Ten”—who were a blacklisted group of writers, directors, and producers that were sent to federal prison—Lardner’s story offers a portrait of political resistance and the cost of personal conviction.[^31] He had attended elite institutions, including Phillips Academy and Princeton University, where his early exposure to the Socialist Club at Princeton University and his time in Russia as an exchange student shaped his progressive worldview, making him more committed to social justice causes as well as against the “smugness and hypocrisy and the hard heart of the world.”[^32] Throughout the 1930s and 1940s, Lardner became increasingly involved with leftist movements, most notably helping to raise funds during the Spanish Civil War for the Popular Front government. He never distanced himself from these commitments, asserting that being leftist was something he “never [had] regretted,” even when it cost him everything.[^33]

Before his political stance brought his career to a halt, Lardner had achieved a successful career in Hollywood. At just the age of twenty-four, he won an Academy Award for Best Original Screenplay for <em>Woman of the Year</em> (1942).[^34] He had also worked on scripts for the films <em>The Cross of Lorraine</em> (1943), <em>Marriage Is a Private Affair</em> (1944), <em>Laura</em> (1944), <em>Brotherhood of Man</em> (1946), <em>Cloak and Dagger</em> (1946) and <em>Forever Amber</em> (1947), but his ascent was suddenly interrupted when he was subpoenaed to testify before HUAC in the fall of 1947.[^35] Refusing to provide names or answer questions about his political affiliations with the Communist Party, Lardner declared, “I could answer the question exactly the way you want, Mr. Chairman… but if I did, I would hate myself in the morning.”[^36] In an interview with Lardner on July 1, 1999, taking place fifty-two years after his testimony, Lardner emphasized that the “[Hollywood Ten] cited the first amendment as [their] reason for not answering these questions and for challenging the committee’s right to ask them.”[^37] This epitomized the moral and ideological stance he took. To him, it was not merely a legal issue but a matter of personal morality. Lardner believed that the committee’s actions were ironic, seen to be a violation of constitutional rights—one that was an affront to the freedom of expression that should have been central to the film industry. In Lardner’s written statement before the committee, though it was ultimately not delivered, he described how Hollywood was a “citadel of freedom” and warned that without the ability to express diverse views in film, the industry would lose creativity, and the “control of their pictures, and the same shackling of education, labor, radio, and newspapers would follow.”[^38] His defiance as a result led to a citation for contempt of Congress and a sentence of a year in federal prison.[^39] Upon his release, Lardner was found blacklisted from all the major studios and ostracized from the mainstream filmmaking world.

It was hard for the “Hollywood Ten” to come back to the industry, as they were in an “entirely different situation” where “nobody would touch [them] as a result of “McCarthy [coming] on the scene, and the hysteria was at its worst.”[^40] For over a whole decade after his return, Lardner was forced to identify himself as a screenwriter using pseudonyms and was unable to claim his own work.[^41] His silence in the face of pressure was not just a political stand but a sacrifice of identity and career to defend the ideals of artistic integrity and the importance of expressive freedom in an industry built on it. Lardner’s story reveals the ways in which the Cold War paranoia suppressed voices that challenged conventional and accepted political views, even as those voices were essential to the diversity and richness of American film during the Golden Age of Hollywood. More than just a victim of the Second Red Scare, Lardner represented a symbol of resilience—a reminder that the Golden Age of Hollywood was more than just about success and charm but also about the fight to preserve freedom of thought and expression in an increasingly conformist society.

In contrast to Lardner’s defense of artistic freedom in film, Gerard David Schine represented the opposite side of the ideological spectrum. Though he emerged from a similar world of privilege and prestige, Schine’s approach was ultimately shaped by fervent anti-Communist ideology. His rise to prominence included his role in fueling the Red Scare—the very movement that sought to suppress the freedoms that individuals like Lardner so thoroughly advocated for. While Schine would eventually become involved in Hollywood as an executive producer of the Academy Award-winning film <em>The French Connection</em> (1971) and as a promoter of music that achieved Billboard gold, platinum, and Cash Box #1 status, his earlier political legacy was rooted in strong anti-communist outlooks.[^42] Similar to Lardner, Schine attended Phillips Academy and, later, Harvard University. He studied Government with a specialization in International Relations, with particular interests that ranged from American foreign policy and international politics to the History of Russia, reflecting his growing concern with global ideological conflict.[^43] During his time at Harvard, Schine took a public political stance when he “produced a six-[page] pamphlet on the dangers of Communism that was distributed free to those staying at his father's hotels,” stating that the Communist Party was guilty of “stealing words, such as freedom, security, and equality from the Bible, and other good covenants to confuse issues, and deceive the mind into ensnarement.”[^44] For Schine, it was during this period that the paranoia about Communism was dangerous, that it could warp and manipulate democracy—and even the English language itself—into “stealing” words like freedom and equality. In effect, he argued that Communism twisted the very foundations of American values, hiding behind language that only seemed noble on the surface. From this perspective, those who have used these terms in opposition to traditional American norms—such as what Lardner had claimed about Hollywood being a place of freedom of expression—might not have been truthfully exercising their rights but participating in deception.[^45]

Schine first started gaining national attention in the early 1950s when he became a consultant to Senator Joseph McCarthy and a close associate of Roy Cohn, McCarthy’s chief counsel.[^46] One of his early assignments in 1953 involved embarking on a tour in Europe to examine libraries of the United States Information Agency for books written by authors they deemed to be Communists.[^47] Schine’s actions illustrated a broader ambition of the Red Scare: not only to control and root out political sympathizers but also the artistic and intellectual environments in which the ideas were formed. In doing so, he helped reinforce the climate of fear and suspicion that silenced political dissent and stifled creative freedom—directly impacting figures like Lardner and other Hollywood figures, shaping the ideological views of postwar America.

Ring Lardner Jr. and Gerard David Schine, despite both being educated at elite institutions like Phillips Academy, represented diametrically opposed perspectives that reflect the larger tensions in Hollywood during the Cold War. Lardner entered Hollywood as a screenwriter during its Golden Age, becoming part of the creative core that defined its cultural and technological prosperity. Schine, on the other hand, came to Hollywood much later after being part of the political landscape that swept the era with the known Second Red Scare. By the time he arrived in the entertainment world, the Golden Age had passed, and Hollywood had already been reshaped by the fear and censorship that figures like him helped enforce. Their distinct contrast in politics accentuates two sides of the same industry: Lardner, the artist whose career was destroyed by the political forces that targeted Hollywood, and Schine, an explicit depiction of these forces that aimed to suppress the voices of expressive freedom. Together, they demonstrate how the Golden Age of Hollywood was not just an accumulation of film and fame but also a side of political conflict—where rising cultural influence met increasing government pressure and where careers could either be made or broken not by one’s talent but by ideology.
